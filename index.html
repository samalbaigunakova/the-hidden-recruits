1. The Problem — When the Recruit Becomes the Criminal

On 30 July 2025, Kazakhstan’s National Bank warned that young people, including teenagers, and pensioners were being drawn into money-mule schemes through social networks and messaging platforms. Recruiters promised easy income for access to payment cards or accounts that would later move suspected criminal proceeds. Crucially, the warning distinguished knowing participation from recruitment without full awareness of the scheme (National Bank of Kazakhstan, 2025a). The distinction matters: the person whose name appears on a bank account may not be the person who designed, controlled, or profited most from the fraud.

Money mules, often called “droppers” in Kazakhstan, provide accounts or transfer funds on another person’s instructions. Some knowingly sell access for payment. Others may believe a message about remote work or a favor from an acquaintance is legitimate. Trust, economic pressure, and unequal information can coexist with responsibility; none automatically establishes innocence. The political question is how institutions identify different roles within a criminal network rather than treating visible transfers as a complete explanation.

A new legal boundary

On 16 September 2025, Article 232-1 of Kazakhstan’s Criminal Code came into force, targeting specified unlawful provision, transfer, or acquisition of access to accounts and payment instruments, alongside unlawful transfers (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2014; Ministry of Internal Affairs of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2025). Its penalties depend on the conduct and aggravating circumstances: some account-access offences carry fines or arrest, aggravated transfer activity may carry imprisonment, and unlawful acquisition of access for illicit transactions under Part 7 carries three to seven years of restriction or deprivation of liberty (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2014). The law also allows an exemption for a first offender who voluntarily reports and actively assists, provided no other offence is constituted (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2014). This safeguard exists on paper; its actual accessibility remains an empirical question.

Enforcement became visible quickly. The Prosecutor General’s Office reported more than 700 cases registered since the new article took effect and, by 29 June 2026, 216 people held criminally accountable, including 202 convicted (Prosecutor General’s Office of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2026). In West Kazakhstan Region, seven 19-year-old college students received three years’ restriction of liberty after opening accounts and giving fraudsters access; more than KZT 4 million of stolen funds passed through those accounts (Prosecutor General’s Office of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2026). The official account does not establish whether they were deceived, pressured, or fully aware. Their age alone cannot answer that question.

The tension is not between protecting fraud victims and protecting offenders. Both matter. Banks and police need to interrupt the flow of stolen money; organizers may rely on identifiable intermediaries who are easier to investigate than remote recruiters. A legal response that counts account holders but cannot explain recruitment risks confusing visibility with control. Equally, assuming all young suspects were exploited would erase agency and harm to victims. The problem is how criminal responsibility is distributed when information, financial bargaining power, and control are uneven. Such distinctions need evidence rather than assumptions about youth.

THE SOCIAL-SCIENCE QUESTION

How does Kazakhstan’s criminal justice system distribute responsibility between fraud organizers and young people recruited to move money, and how do economic vulnerability, deception, and enforcement practices shape who faces prosecution?

2. Background & Data — What the Numbers Cannot Explain

Kazakhstan now records money-mule offences under a distinct criminal-law provision, but administrative counts primarily describe detection and enforcement, not recruitment prevalence. The Committee on Legal Statistics and Special Accounts (CPS&SA) manages criminal records through Qamqor; the Prosecutor General’s Office and Ministry of Internal Affairs publish case summaries (Prosecutor General’s Office of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2026; Committee on Legal Statistics and Special Accounts, n.d.). The National Bank’s Anti-Fraud Centre tracks suspicious financial incidents, while the Bureau of National Statistics of the Republic of Kazakhstan measures employment and other social conditions (Bureau of National Statistics of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2026; National Bank of Kazakhstan, 2026). These datasets have different units and cannot be merged into a single count of “money mules.” Administrative visibility should not be mistaken for the underlying population of people recruited, approached, or harmed.

Internationally, INTERPOL and Europol document recruitment through job offers, social media, relationships, and impersonation (INTERPOL, n.d.; Europol, n.d.). Their typologies help identify mechanisms, not Kazakhstan’s national prevalence. No reviewed open dataset links an individual’s recruitment message, account activity, knowledge, judicial outcome, and access to the statutory exemption.

The generational bridge is striking: young adults predominate among recorded suspects, while people older than 30 predominate among recognized victims. But the 214 individuals were suspects in completed proceedings, not a representative sample of everyone approached by recruiters. The 461 victims belong to those completed cases, not every financial-fraud victim nationwide.

Where the data goes silent

1. The legal break. Article 232-1 began in September 2025. A rise in registrations may reflect changed classification, investigations, and reporting. Offences, cases, suspects, and convictions are not interchangeable measures; none gives a population-level recruitment rate.

2. Selection into enforcement. Data on completed investigations omit undetected schemes, ongoing investigations, people who refused recruitment, and unprosecuted organizers. Visible account holders may be easier to identify than those who control payments remotely. The recorded age distribution could partly reflect that selection.

3. Vulnerability without causation. The 159 young suspects and 140 unemployed suspects are separate totals; their overlap is unpublished. Neither youth nor unemployment proves deception or causation. The national youth unemployment rate was 2.9% for ages 15–34 in Q3 2026 (Bureau of National Statistics of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2026), a different population and date, unsuitable for a direct risk comparison.

4. Missing intent and safeguards. Aggregate records do not describe recruitment messages, promised pay, threats, understanding, or who controlled the funds. They do not disclose how frequently first-time offenders sought or received Article 232-1’s voluntary-reporting exemption, or how sanctions differed between organizers and intermediaries. Without linked, privacy-protected case information, claims of proportionate enforcement remain difficult to test.

The statistics count the people detected and the harm documented. They are far less able to explain how one group becomes involved in moving funds while another loses them. That is why criminal-network, behavioral, and technical research must be read together.

3. Current State of Research — Detection Is Not Explanation

Section 2 uncovered a generational bridge: 74.3% of recorded suspects were aged 18–29, while 75.9% of recognized victims were older than 30. The groups are not matched pairs, but the contrast raises a question beyond criminal counts: how do people become intermediaries in schemes that harm others? Three academic traditions answer different parts of it. Criminological research maps network power; behavioral research studies exposure and recruitment; technical research recognizes suspicious financial patterns. Their differing samples determine what each can claim. Comparing methods is therefore substantive, not decorative: it tells us whose experiences become visible to researchers and whose decisions remain outside the evidence.

Criminal networks: participation is not equal control

Bekkers, Leukfeldt, and Kleemans (2024) examined 15 closed police investigations in the Netherlands and distinguished organizers, professional facilitators, recruited facilitators, and money mules. Intermediaries were approached through social media and offline relationships; some received money, while others reportedly faced manipulation or threats. The finding challenges a flat view of every participant as equally informed. It does not establish how often coercion occurs among all recruits because the evidence begins with networks already investigated.

Roks, Leukfeldt, and Densley (2021) similarly show that offline street networks and online offending intertwine. A Telegram message may be embedded in a prior friendship or local relationship. For Kazakhstan, Temirbolat, Urkumbay, and Alshurazova (2026) identify gaps in how domestic law addresses phishing, vishing, and social engineering. Their comparative legal analysis gives institutional context, but it does not evaluate the application of Article 232-1 to money-mule recruits. Network studies explain organization better than the unseen pool of people who refuse an approach.

Behavioral research: three different observational windows

The Dutch Bekkers research cluster is especially revealing methodologically. Its projects are not a longitudinal study of the same people, nor were they published in a simple linear sequence. Yet they deliberately open three observational windows. The 2024 network study uses closed police files: it observes detected participation. Bekkers, Moneva, and Leukfeldt (2024) used a field quasi-experiment with simulated recruitment advertisements on Instagram: up to 3% of targeted young users engaged, particularly with luxury-oriented or neutralizing messages. That measures engagement, not willingness to transfer stolen funds. Bekkers, Spithoven, Van Houten, and Leukfeldt (2025) then surveyed about 2,000 Dutch people aged 16–25 about previous recruiter contact. This captures exposure, including people not arrested or known to police, but depends on respondents’ recollection and cannot prove subsequent participation.

The comparison with Hong and Lee (2024) is instructive. Their South Korean study models recruitment channels among 543 apprehended money mules. Unemployed people in their twenties with certain educational profiles were more likely to appear in particular online recruitment channels. That is useful for understanding detected offenders, but selection starts after apprehension. We cannot learn from it how many similarly situated young adults were contacted and refused, or whether unemployment caused involvement. The Dutch survey broadens the denominator; the Instagram study tests attraction; the police and Korean samples describe selected cases. None alone measures a pathway from financial stress to criminal intent.

This matters when interpreting Kazakhstan’s 140 unemployed suspects. A high percentage among completed cases might reflect targeted recruitment, financial precarity, detection practices, or all three. To claim exploitation from employment status alone would substitute an appealing narrative for causal evidence. Conversely, dismissing vulnerability because some recruits receive payment would ignore unequal information and control.

Technical research: detecting a pattern is not judging a person

Vorobyev and Krivitskaya (2022) designed rule-induction methods to reduce false positives in bank anti-fraud screening, drawing on a large transaction dataset. Asomura, Iijima, and Mori (2023) tested detection of unauthorized transfers in Japanese online banking and emphasized heavy manual-review burdens when legitimate transactions trigger alarms. Their contribution is operational: improved screening may reduce investigative delay and wasted review time. But the ground truth is a transaction label, not a judicial finding about the account holder’s knowledge, bargaining power, or role in a network.

The distinction cuts both ways. An accurate alert can identify fraud-associated activity without explaining whether its apparent owner was deceived. An inaccurate alert can burden an innocent account holder, particularly someone dependent on immediate access to funds. Neither technical accuracy nor a youth demographic correlation is a substitute for procedural fairness. The literature also offers few controlled estimates of whether a warning delivered before recruitment reduces later money-mule involvement.

03 / RESEARCH GAP

THE GAP

Research explains how illicit networks recruit, which young people encounter offers, and how banks detect suspect transactions. What remains unresolved in Kazakhstan is how these mechanisms meet under Article 232-1: who was deceived, who controlled the scheme, how institutions distinguish responsibility, and whether voluntary prevention can reduce recruitment before criminal liability becomes a possibility. A defensible intervention must test prevention without turning suspicion into punishment.

4. The AI-Assisted Solution — and What It Would Cost

From detection to prevention

Section 3’s conclusion is a design constraint: suspicious transfers are not explanations of responsibility. A system that merely finds more young account holders could amplify the enforcement imbalance identified in Section 2. The priority is recognizing recruitment before money moves; bank detection remains a bounded research component. The proposed pilot therefore separates a voluntary bilingual warning service from a bank-side model that can be evaluated without independently changing customers’ rights.

The architecture: two layers, different powers

Layer 1 is a free Kazakh/Russian recruitment checker for students and other job-seekers. Users choose to paste a Telegram or Instagram offer or a payment request, ideally after redacting identifying information. A small language model and reviewed guidance identify warning signs—unrealistic earnings, sharing account access, or receiving and forwarding third-party funds—and explain why each matters. It explains Article 232-1, the conditional exemption, and verified next steps (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2014; National Bank of Kazakhstan, 2025a). It never declares guilt, promises immunity, or certifies an apparently safe message. No login or automatic police report is required.

Layer 2 is a separate bank-side model using data already lawfully available to two participating banks. During a 12-month pilot, an alert enters a 24-hour shadow-mode observation and human evaluation window: the model records a risk score for analysis, but cannot independently pause transfers, freeze an account, or send a criminal referral. Existing anti-fraud obligations continue unchanged. Article 25-1 of the Law on Payments and Payment Systems provides existing procedures for temporary suspension of suspicious transactions for up to five working days, and the National Bank reports continued access to protected wages, pensions, stipends, and benefits under relevant restrictions (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2016; National Bank of Kazakhstan, 2025b). The pilot grants no new suspension power. Banks remain legally responsible for decisions; model developers and evaluators do not replace them.

Voluntary checker inquiries must never create bank or police suspicion. University partners can distribute the checker and verified advice, but must not collect students’ individual queries. The pilot tests whether preventative explanations add value and whether shadow scoring could someday improve existing investigations, not whether AI can automatically identify criminals.

What two real implementations establish

Singapore’s government-backed ScamShield checks or flags suspicious communications and uses user reports to improve protection (Open Government Products, 2026). Its Q3 2026 dashboard reported 4.39 million scam alerts, 86% precision, 85.8% recall, and approximately SGD 724,200 in quarterly operating costs. Those are published operational measures, not proof of crimes prevented. A trial of scam-trend notifications was rolled back because reach was low and increased protective behavior could not be established. This is a warning against assuming that exposure to advice changes behavior.

UnionBank in the Philippines developed ML detection using account-opening and transaction data (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2024). The OECD reports an initial 42% detection rate for confirmed money mules, 90% accuracy when predicting non-mules, and identification of some mule accounts four days after opening rather than about a month through traditional investigation. These metrics neither establish knowledge nor justify automatic restrictions. Together the cases justify testing a public warning service and earlier identification, but not importing their performance to Kazakhstan or presuming fewer prosecutions.

The bill, II — Economic: the financing dilemma

A two-bank, three-university-partner pilot is provisionally priced at KZT 138.7 million in Year 1: KZT 46.0 million in setup and KZT 92.7 million in annual operations, including contingency. This is a transparent planning scenario, not procurement evidence. Commercial banks may resist paying for a service that protects citizens across institutions while producing thousands of extra manual alerts. That collective-action problem makes governance part of the bill. The National Bank’s existing Anti-Fraud Centre could coordinate a co-financed consumer-protection pilot, subject to appropriate authorization and agreement; mandatory bank participation would require a specific legal basis. Neither funding model currently exists for this proposal. Banks would gain fewer fraud losses, while young users and potential victims receive much of the benefit. Independent evaluation must compare the intervention against cheaper university campaigns, ordinary bank warnings and rule-based screening.

The bill, III — Labor and error

Automation reallocates work instead of erasing it. Paid bilingual annotators must check evolving scam language; legal specialists must update statutory explanations; security engineers maintain the service; and bank compliance staff assess alerts. At two million transactions, even a small flag rate creates substantial review work. Assuming 20 minutes per alert and 1,600 productive reviewer hours annually, a 0.2% alert rate creates 4,000 reviews, approximately 1,333 hours or 0.8 full-time equivalent; a 1% alert rate creates 20,000 reviews, approximately 6,667 hours or 4.2 FTE. The two funded reviewers cannot absorb the higher scenario. Worker strain and wrongful suspicion remain costs.

The bill, IV — Political power and governance

Kazakhstan’s AI Law No. 230-VIII, in force from January 2026, makes rights and accountability central rather than optional. Articles 7–9 address explanation, operator responsibility and human autonomy; Articles 17–18 concern risk classification and management; Articles 20–21 address audits and disclosure obligations (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2025). The public checker and bank model require distinct risk assessments. Bank scoring warrants especially close scrutiny because future deployment could affect access to essential financial services. Classification must be determined under applicable rules, not asserted by the project. Age or unemployment must never independently establish suspicion. The central political risk is function creep: bank signals could later support generalized youth profiling, law-enforcement access or unrelated commercial decisions. Model owners, banks, the regulator, and independent evaluators therefore need clearly separated powers, audit trails, and limits on secondary use.

The bill, V — Data ethics

Pasted chat screenshots may expose names, phone numbers, account details, and private third parties. Article 12 of Kazakhstan’s Personal Data Law requires personal-data storage in databases or digital objects located in Kazakhstan, alongside other processing safeguards (Republic of Kazakhstan, 2013). The checker should default to anonymous use, redact or discourage identifying details, minimize retention, and never silently train on submissions. Consent to processing is not consent to police referral; a low-risk model response is not a guarantee. Banks keep transaction records within lawful systems, separate from voluntary inquiries. False positives may produce suspicion and administrative burdens even in shadow mode, while false negatives may create dangerous reassurance. Audit error rates in both languages; give affected individuals accessible human remedies whenever a consequential decision is made.

Verdict: a conditional yes, or no to expansion

Kazakhstan should test the voluntary checker and tightly restricted shadow model only if five conditions hold: (1) no AI-triggered punishment, freezes, or criminal referrals; (2) legally accountable human review and accessible redress; (3) strict separation of voluntary queries from banking and policing data; (4) evidence that warnings and detection add benefit beyond simpler alternatives, including disaggregated published false-positive results; and (5) audited labor, budget, energy, and water costs with an enforceable shutdown rule. If consent, oversight, or meaningful evaluation cannot be guaranteed, reject the bank expansion. Fund ordinary financial education, verified public warnings, trained human assistance and better investigations of recruiters instead. The goal is fewer people recruited into financial crime, not merely more people identified by an algorithm.
